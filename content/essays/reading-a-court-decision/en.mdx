Court decisions are public documents, and more of them are freely available online than ever. Yet most people meet them only through a headline: *court rules against government*. The decision itself is rarely read. It should be, and it is less forbidding than it looks.

## Find the question

Every judgment answers a question, and the first task is to find it. It is often narrower than the news suggests. A court may not have decided whether a policy is good, or even whether it is lawful in general, but only whether a particular procedure was followed in a particular case.

## Separate the holding from the reasoning

The **holding** is what the court decided. The **reasoning** is why. Both matter, but differently. The holding binds the parties. The reasoning is what later courts, lawyers and citizens will argue about — and it frequently contains limits that the holding alone does not reveal.

> The holding is what the court decided. The reasoning is why — and it often contains the limits.

## Read the dissent

Where judges disagree, the dissenting opinion is often the clearest statement of what was really at stake. It can also be a message to the future: some dissents have later become the law.

## Four questions to ask

1. What exactly was the court asked to decide?
2. What facts did it treat as established?
3. What rule did it apply, and where did the rule come from?
4. What did it expressly *not* decide?

A reader who can answer those four questions knows more about the case than most of the coverage it receives.
