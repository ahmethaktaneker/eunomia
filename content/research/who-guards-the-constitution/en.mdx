## The question

Every written constitution claims to be higher law. That claim is only meaningful if some institution can declare an ordinary law invalid for conflicting with it. The question this note asks is simple: which institution, and why?

## Method

This is a comparative, doctrinal note. It draws on the founding texts and landmark decisions of the two models and does not attempt a quantitative comparison of outcomes.

## The decentralised model

In *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), the United States Supreme Court held that it was "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is", including when a statute conflicts with the Constitution.[^1] The power that followed belongs, in principle, to every court: any judge hearing a case may decline to apply an unconstitutional law, subject to appeal.

## The centralised model

The Austrian Constitution of 1920, shaped by the legal theorist Hans Kelsen, took a different path. It created a single Constitutional Court with the exclusive power to annul laws.[^2] Ordinary judges could not set aside legislation themselves. The model spread widely after the Second World War: Germany's Federal Constitutional Court began work in 1951, and Turkey's Constitutional Court was established under the 1961 Constitution.

## Trade-offs

Concentration brings consistency and expertise; it also makes a single body an attractive target for political capture. Dispersal makes capture harder but can produce slow, uneven answers. Neither model settles the deeper question of independence, which depends on appointments, tenure and the willingness of other institutions to comply.

## Limits

This note describes ideal types. Most real systems mix features of both, and practice often diverges from design.

[^1]: *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. 137 (1803).
[^2]: Bundes-Verfassungsgesetz (Austria), 1920; see Hans Kelsen's writings on the guarantee of the constitution.
